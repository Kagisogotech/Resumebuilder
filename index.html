What actually gets CVs thrown out

An applicant tracking system reads your CV as text before a recruiter sees it. These are the failures that cost people interviews, in rough order of how often they happen.

A PDF that is secretly a picture

Plenty of CV tools render your PDF as an image. It looks perfect and contains no readable text, so it scores zero. Our PDF is typeset as real text — open it and press Ctrl+F.

Two columns and sidebars

Parsers read straight across the page. A sidebar interleaves with your experience and turns your career history into nonsense. Every template here is a single column.

Tables and text boxes

A Word CV laid out in a table often extracts as a jumble, or as nothing. Same for headers and footers — contact details put there routinely vanish.

Incomplete dates

Without a start and end month the system cannot compute your years of experience — so it often discards the role, then filters you out for lacking experience you have.

Invented section names

"My Journey" is not a heading any parser knows. Call it Work Experience. Creativity in the headings costs you the whole section.

"Responsible for…"

This one passes the parser and fails the human. A bullet that describes duties instead of results is the difference between a CV that is read and one that is skimmed.

What you get

Upload the CV you already have

Drop in a PDF, Word file or plain text. It is read in your browser — the text is extracted, split into sections, and rebuilt as a clean single-column CV. You get a full report of every change and a list of what the importer was unsure about, because a heuristic that pretends to be certain is worse than one that admits doubt.

A rewrite that fixes form, never facts

"Was responsible for the management of a team" becomes "Managed a team". First-person pronouns, filler openers, self-rated star ratings and duplicate skills all go. What it will not do is invent a number, a tool or an employer — you sign your name to this document. Where a bullet needs a figure, it tells you and leaves the writing to you.

A score that tells you what to fix

Seven weighted categories, scored out of 100, updating as you type: contact completeness, required sections, content depth, quantified results, phrasing, format hygiene and skills breadth. Every finding comes with the specific fix, ordered by what costs you the most.

Match against a specific advert

Paste a job ad and see which of its terms are missing from your CV, with anything under a "requirements" heading weighted higher. Multi-word skills are matched as phrases, so "machine learning" counts once rather than as two meaningless words.

A profile, and as many CVs as you need

Sign in with an email address or Google and your CVs follow you to every device, surviving a cleared browser or a new laptop. Keep a tailored CV per role — targeting each application beats one generic CV every time. Or skip the account entirely: without one, everything still works and stays in this browser. Either way you can rename, duplicate, delete and download a backup.

Exports that are actually readable

PDF with a genuine text layer. Word files as real Word XML rather than HTML in a .docx wrapper — which is what most browser-based builders quietly produce, and what the libraries ATS vendors parse with read as an empty document. Plus plain text for paste-in-a-box forms.

Where your CV lives

That depends on one choice, and it is worth being precise about it.

Without an account — the default — your CVs stay in this browser on this device and are never uploaded. Reading your file, scoring it, rewriting it and generating every export all happen in this tab. Nothing is sent anywhere. The catch is the obvious one: clearing your browser data deletes them, and they do not follow you to another device.

With an account, your CVs are stored in Google Firebase so they reach your phone and your other computers, and survive a wiped browser. That is a genuine trade: the data now sits on Google's servers rather than only on your machine. Only you can read it — the database rules restrict every document to the account that owns it — but it is no longer purely local, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. You can delete the account and everything in it at any time.

Either way: a CV is not a secret document, but it does carry your name, address and phone number. Keep ID numbers, banking details and anything genuinely sensitive out of it — and off the version you email to strangers.
